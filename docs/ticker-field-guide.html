The short version

One small server that you own, talking to three services you rent

Almost everything is a single program running on a single machine. It fetches prices, keeps your transaction history, serves the web page, and talks to the AI. Splitting that across more machines would make it worse, not better — the reasons are further down.

What it can't do alone is know what stocks cost and be reachable from the internet. That's what the outside services are for. Each one does exactly one job, and each can be swapped without touching the rest.

Signal path

How a price reaches your screen

Source The exchange A trade happens. NASDAQ knows about it instantly; you don't.
Rented Finnhub Sells access to that firehose. Sends every trade down one open connection.
Yours The price hub Catches hundreds of trades a second and forwards at most four updates a second.
Yours Your browser The tape flips to the new number. No refresh, no polling.

The third box is the one that matters. A busy stock like NVDA prints hundreds of trades per second; a browser asked to redraw that many times simply locks up. So the server remembers the newest price for each symbol and sends a batch every 250 milliseconds. You see a live number, and the tab stays responsive.

Rented

The four outside services, and what each is for

Finnhub — the price feed

Free tier
Does what
Supplies live prices and lets you search for a ticker by company name. It holds one permanently open connection to the market and pushes every trade through it.
Why this one
It was the only free service offering genuinely real-time US stock prices over a push connection. Most free tiers give you delayed prices, or make you ask repeatedly instead of being told.
The catch
Its free tier stops at live prices. Historical charts are a paid feature — we found that only after deploying, when the request came back refused.
Without it
No live prices. Your portfolio still shows what you paid and what you've made on sales, because that comes from your own records.

Twelve Data — the history

Free tier
Does what
Supplies past prices — the daily closes behind both charts. Nothing live; it answers questions about yesterday and last year.
Why it exists
Purely to fill Finnhub's gap. Rather than pay Finnhub for history, the app asks a second service for that one thing. Prices still come from Finnhub.
Why this one
Its free tier allows 800 requests a day. The obvious alternative allows 25 — six clicks on the chart's range buttons would exhaust a day's quota.
Without it
Both charts go blank and say so. Everything else is unaffected.

Anthropic — the analyst

Pay per use
Does what
Powers the chat panel. It can look up your holdings, check a price, read your transaction history, and search the web for news — then answer in plain language.
The important part
It can propose changes to your portfolio but cannot make them. "Add 10 shares of MSFT" produces a confirmation card; nothing is written until you click Confirm.
Why that matters
Not distrust of the model. "Sell everything," typed at 2am, should meet a confirmation dialog — the same as any other destructive action.
Without it
The chat panel is unavailable. The dashboard works normally.

Fly.io — where it lives

~$3/month
Does what
Runs the app on a small always-on computer with a public web address and an HTTPS certificate. It's the landlord, not a component — it hosts the thing the other three talk to.
Why this one
It keeps a long-lived connection open, which the price feed depends on. Many cheap hosts shut an app down between visits — that would kill the market connection every time you looked away.
Also provides
A volume: a small disk that survives restarts. Your transaction history lives there. Without it, every deploy would wipe your portfolio.
Without it
The app runs on your laptop, reachable only by you, only while the laptop is awake.

Yours

The parts nobody rents you

Back end FastAPI Python. Holds the market connection open, serves the web page, runs the chat. One program doing every job.
Front end React The dashboard itself — tape, tables, charts, chat. Built into plain files the server hands out.
Storage SQLite A database that is one file on the disk. For one person's trades, a database server would be pure overhead.

Decisions

Four choices that shaped everything else

  • Exactly one machine — on purpose
    Running two copies would open two connections to the market, and the price feed allows one. They'd also each keep their own copy of your portfolio and drift apart. This is a correctness constraint, not a way to save money.
  • Your transaction list is the only truth
    Nothing else is stored. Your current holdings, average cost and profit are all calculated from the list of buys and sells, every time. Records are only ever added, never edited — so the numbers can always be rebuilt from scratch and checked.
  • The AI proposes; you decide
    There is no route the model can take that writes to your records. It can only prepare a confirmation card. The single line of code that saves a trade runs when you click Confirm, and nowhere else.
  • It reports, it never advises
    The analyst is instructed never to recommend buying or selling, never to give price targets, never to predict. Asked "should I sell my AAPL?", it declines and offers data instead. Every answer carries a disclaimer.
